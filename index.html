Call it

A song is a few sections in a row: an intro, a verse, a chorus that comes back. Each coloured strip above the bars is one section. Tap a strip to pick it — while the music plays, the song jumps there at the next bar. Duplicate makes a copy next to it. Own parts gives the copy its own drums and notes, so you can change it without changing the original. Keys 1 to 8 jump to a section too.

Each row is one drum sound. The squares across are moments, played in order from left to right, then it loops. Switch a square on and that sound happens at that moment.

Mood

Swaps which notes exist. Your pattern stays; the feeling changes.

Music gets its feeling from which notes are allowed. Sad uses a minor scale, Happy a major one. You are not learning names yet — you are hearing the difference that makes.

Key C

The key is the note your song calls home. Moving it slides every note up or down together, so the tune stays the same but sits higher or lower. Handy when a song is too high or too low to sing along to.

Real note names

Swap “low 1, mid 2” for what musicians actually call them.

Every note has a letter name from A to G, and a small ♭ means slightly lower. The same seven letters repeat as you climb, which is why C shows up twice on the grid — the higher one is the same note, an octave up.

Sounds

Pick which instrument each track plays. Tap one to hear it.

Every sound in here is built out of pure tones rather than recordings, which is why the whole thing still works with the wifi off. Changing an instrument does not change your notes — the same tune just gets played by something else.

What are you going for?

Pick a style and the helper compares your song to it. Nothing changes unless you tap a Try button.

A style (musicians say genre) is a set of habits that lots of songs share: how fast, how busy, which sounds. They are habits, not rules, and plenty of well-loved songs ignore them. Picking one gives the helper something to measure your song against. “No style, my own thing” switches all of this off.

    Sounds that go with this

    Pads

    Eight pads. Each one holds a short sound. Tap a pad or press its letter key to play it.

    A pad is a button that holds one sound, like the sampler on a DJ deck. Capture this bar copies the bar that is playing (or the bar you last tapped in the track) onto the next empty pad. Chop it onto the pads cuts one of your recordings into eight equal pieces — a slicer — so a sentence becomes eight sounds you can play in any order. Tap an empty pad to put one drum or one note on it. With Play in time on, a pad waits for the next square of the song, so it lands in time (quantise). Pads are for playing: they are not written into the grid and they are not in the file when you save the song. Record a live take in the Mix area records what comes out of the speakers, so it keeps your pad playing too. Pad sounds stay on this device. Teach a button: with a USB instrument plugged in, tap a pad, then hit a button on the instrument, and that button plays that pad from then on. The live effects in the Mix area can be taught the same way.

    Swap in a ready-made idea

    The song turns a knob

    Pick one setting, then draw how high it is in each bar. Drag across the bars.

    This is called automation: the song turning its own knobs. At the start of every bar the setting glides to the height you drew for that bar. The classic use is closing Brightness during a verse and opening it for the chorus, so the chorus feels like a door opening. While a setting is drawn here its knob shows a small “auto” tag and the song is in charge of it. Pick Off and the knob is in charge again. Flat puts every bar back at the knob’s own value.

    Mixer

    Level is how loud a track is. Tone makes it darker (left) or thinner (right).

    Balance is most of what a producer does. If everything is at full volume nothing stands out. Try pulling a layer down rather than pushing another up. The Tone knob is a filter: turn it left and the bright top of the sound goes away, turn it right and the low end goes away. Leave it in the middle and it does nothing.

    Groove

    Nudges every second square late, so it stops sounding like a machine.

    This is called swing. Straight is exact and mechanical; swung is how most blues, jazz, hip hop and house actually feel. Real drummers do it without thinking.

    Swing0%

    Studio knobs

    Three knobs that change how everything sounds. Drag up and down.

    Brightness takes the high frequencies away, so the music sounds like it is behind a door. Space adds the echo of a room. Echo repeats what you play. Producers use all three constantly — turning them down is as useful as turning them up.

    Live effects

    Press and hold while the music plays. Let go and it snaps back.

    These are for playing your song like a DJ would. Echo throws everything into repeats. Stutter repeats the half-beat you pressed it on. Muffle sounds like closing a door on the music. Build-up thins the sound out with a rising hiss, and lands with a kick when you let go. Echo out stops the song and lets the last sound ring away, which is a good ending. None of these change your song — record a live take if you want to keep what you played.

    Record & import

    Three tracks for your own sounds. Sing, beatbox, or bring a file in.

    Recording gives you one bar of count-in clicks and then runs for exactly the length of your song, so it loops with everything else. Anything you record or import goes through the knobs too. Press Play it as an instrument and that sound becomes playable from the note grid — record yourself saying one word and you can write a whole tune out of it.

    Your own instruments

    Everything you record stays on this device. It is never sent anywhere, and it is not in anything you share.

    Keep everything safe
      
    
    
      Your songs live in this browser on this device. A backup is one file with every song, every earlier version and every recording in it. Keep it somewhere safe, or open it on another device to move everything across.
      
    
      Browsers sometimes clear what a website has stored, for example when the device runs low on space or someone clears the browsing data. A backup file cannot be cleared that way. Bringing a backup in never deletes anything that is already here — it only adds.
